# ABSTRACT 

Large Language Models are transforming the support for clinical decision and their application in real scenarios. Yet, most benchmarks are conducted in English, and cross-lingual evaluation is needed to tackle the language gaps in global access. We introduce ClinicalBr, the first bilingual benchmark for clinical decision built from real Brazilian case reports. The corpus contains 2,892 cases drawn from 28 SciELO medical journals, spanning 18 specialties, and is structured as parallel Portuguese–English pairs. Each case supports four evaluation tasks: diagnosis retrieval, differential diagnosis, exam recommendation, and treatment planning. We evaluate four models: MedGemma-27B, Sabiá-4, DeepSeek-R1, and o3-mini, across both languages. The central finding is that the Portuguese–English performance gap is task-dependent, not general. In diagnosis retrieval, English yields a consistent advantage across all models, with +7.5–12.1 accuracy points. This advantage disappears in differential diagnosis, exam recommendation, and treatment planning, where confidence intervals cross zero for most models and Portuguese completeness scores are marginally higher. Brazilian-endemic conditions proved easier than the full corpus, not harder, indicating that tropical presentations are adequately represented in current pre-training. Exam recommendation was the hardest task across all models and both languages, with F1 scores below 0.10, well below the differential diagnosis ceiling of 0.20–0.27. 

Keywords large language models · cross-lingual evaluation · clinical Decision Support · medical benchmarks 

# 1 Introduction 

The advancement of LLMs has shown increasing capabilities in healthcare, improving natural language understanding to aid clinical decision-making [ 1, 2 , 3]. Language models can decompose problems step by step and, on specific benchmark tasks, match or surpass clinicians. Because these models encode vast clinical knowledge, they perform well on structured clinical decision tasks such as diagnosis and question answering. However, this performance has been established almost entirely in English. The benchmarks that define the field were draw from English-language licensing exams [4], while most clinical care worldwide occurs in other languages, on records written in those languages. Most existing medical benchmarks are English-language and built on licensing-exam multiple-choice questions [ 5, 6, 7 , 8, 9]. Both choices limit what such benchmarks can measure. Licensing exams test textbook knowledge against a fixed answer key, but clinical practice is local: disease prevalence, available diagnostic tests, and treatment guidelines differ by country. A model that scores well on US-derived exams has not thereby been shown to reason over the epidemiological profile and clinical constraints a physician faces elsewhere. In the Brazilian context, a few Portuguese-language medical benchmarks have recently appeared [ 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 ], but they largely inherit the same exam-derived, multiple-choice format and so leave the central question — whether models reason over local clinical cases or only retrieve familiar facts — unanswered. Cross-lingual evaluation is needed because language gaps are tied to fairness and global access. Models are still trained mainly on high-resource languages, which leaves them less prepared for mid- and low-resource languages that are important in real-world care. Recent work links these gaps to broader corpus fairness problems, including geographic, cultural, and socio-economic bias [ 15 , 16 ]. For these reasons, multilingual healthcare benchmarks are not just a technical nice-to-have; they are a basic requirement for valid evaluation. New benchmark efforts are explicitly motivated by the need to reflect the multilingual reality of medicine, broaden coverage beyond English and beyond narrow text-only tasks, and better judge whether medical LLMs are safe and useful across populations rather than only in English-speaking contexts [2, 17] To adress this, we proposed ClinicalBr, a benchmark designed to evaluate the clinical capabilites of LLMs within the Brazilian Context. ClinicalBr pairs 2,892 real Brazilian case reports in Portuguese and English across four open-ended clinical tasks, diagnosis retrieval, differential diagnosis, exam recommendation, and treatment planning. Among all cases, we span more than 15 types of bodies and disorders, with 219 cases focused on Tropical Diseases. ClinicalBr is primarily focused on reflecting Brazilian cases and guidelines. We make three contributions: • We show that the cross-lingual performance gap in clinical LLMs is confined to single-answer retrieval and disappears under structured reasoning, a dissociation the prevailing English multiple-choice benchmarks cannot detect. • We show this pattern is consistent with knowledge overlap rather than reasoning ability: the gap tracks how retrieval-anchored a model is, and conditions sparse in English corpora do not behave as a knowledge deficit would predict. • We release ClinicalBR, a bilingual PT/EN benchmark of real Brazilian case reports spanning four open-ended clinical tasks, together with its paired-difference evaluation protocol, for measuring cross-lingual clinical competence. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Medical Benchmarks for LLMs 

The evaluation of LLM efficacy in medicine has evolved from static knowledge testing toward the analysis of complex clinical competencies. Traditionally, performance was measured through medical licensing examinations, such as the USMLE and CHMLE, which form the basis of widely cited datasets like MedQA and MedMCQA [ 18 , 19 ]. This approach was consolidated by benchmark families such as MultiMedQA, which combines multiple sources of professional, research, and consumer health questions to test medical competence in a manner analogous to student assessment [ 3 ]. Although effective for measuring the mastery of standardized facts, these multiple-choice-based benchmarks have shown limitations, especially with the emergence of advanced reasoning models that have rendered these traditional challenges less rigorous [20]. In light of this saturation, recent research has shifted toward benchmarks that prioritize clinical reasoning in real-world scenarios, moving away from an exclusive reliance on academic exams. Consequently, platforms focused on specific subdomains have emerged—such as medical calculators and X-ray visual interpretation—alongside denser datasets like Medbullets and RareArena, which utilize broader data sources to simulate daily clinical practice [ 21 ]. In this context, it is observed that the failure of traditional benchmarks lies in the insufficient evaluation of diagnostic capability, which requires complex reasoning over multidimensional patient records. To fill this gap, DiagnosisArena has emerged as a proposal designed to comprehensively evaluate diagnostic reasoning within simulated clinical environments [6]. Parallel to this, medical evaluation has expanded beyond the question-and-answer (QA) format, encompassing tasks such as clinical summarization, information extraction, and predictive decision support. Resources such as MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and ClinicalBench allow models to be tested on practical tasks, including treatment recommendations and report generation [ 22 ]. Finally, it is common practice to also evaluate medical models on general benchmarks, such as MMLU-Pro, to ensure that refinement in a specialized domain does not compromise the model’s general logic and reasoning capabilities [9]. 2ClinicalBr 

2.2 LLMs applied to Healthcare 

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) in the healthcare sector has resulted in a shift towards a more interactive and reasoning-based clinical AI, moving beyond traditional algorithms to dynamic systems capable of answering complex medical questions and retrieving specialized knowledge. Specialized models, such as Med-PaLM 2, have demonstrated the ability to handle professional-level medical examinations and provide detailed explanations for physicians, students, and patients [ 3 , 23 ]. This capability extends to clinical decision support, where LLMs are increasingly used to suggest diagnoses, optimize treatment plans, and reason about patient cases through the analysis of longitudinal data from Electronic Health Records (EHR) [ 24 , 25 ]. By mimicking human clinical reasoning, these models assist in transforming raw data into actionable insights, although researchers argue that their current role is better suited for supervised assistance than autonomous practice, due to ongoing concerns about factual grounding and safety [26, 27]. Beyond direct clinical reasoning, LLMs have become important in optimizing the administrative and documentation burdens that characterize modern medicine. One of the most successful applications involves the automated sum-marization of physician-patient dialogs, radiology reports, and discharge records, often producing results that expert reviewers judge to be superior to human-written notes in terms of clarity and conciseness [ 28 , 29 ]. Furthermore, these models serve as a bridge between unstructured clinical narratives and structured data systems, performing automated ICD coding and medical named entity recognition [ 30 ]. This structured extraction is vital for hospital management and large-scale biomedical research, enabling the synthesis of vast amounts of literature and clinical trial data into living systematic reviews [ 31 ]. The future trend in this area points toward multimodal medical foundation models that integrate diverse data streams, including text, medical images, and genomics [ 32 , 33 ]. These generalist systems are being developed to perform sophisticated tasks, such as the interpretation of chest X-rays and cross-modal data retrieval, effectively acting as comprehensive assistants across various medical specialties [ 34 , 35 ]. Additionally, LLMs are expanding their role into patient-facing functions, providing health education, triage support, and mental health assistance, thereby increasing accessibility to medical knowledge [ 36 ]. However, as these models move closer to routine clinical care, the literature emphasizes that addressing ethical challenges — specifically regarding algorithmic bias, data privacy, and the potential for hallucinations — remains a prerequisite for their widespread adoption [37].

3.1 Corpus construction 

The first phase for discovering all the documents to parse was to find the ID of all the 28 medical related journals. After finding the IDs, we used the SciELO ArticleMeta API to fetch all the metadata from the papers. Article metadata was filtered to the case-report document type and year, and the full text was retrieved through Firecrawl [ 38 ]. A 2 stage filtered then happeneded to improve the quality of the corpus. Stage 1 filtered each article as a clinical case report related to the diagnosis or therapeutic process with the Prompt 9.1. As we screened all the cases, multi-patient cases were identified. To prevent the inclusion of cases that could mislead the results of the LLMs, we did the Stage 2 of filtering using an LLM with the Prompt 9.4. Afterall, all cases were classified in thier predoment type between diagnosis, treatment and diagnosis-treatment (both) with the Prompt 9.1 in the end we had 853 diagnosis cases, 744 therapeutic cases and 1,304. Per-stage counts and the type distribution of the resulting corpus are in Table 1. Every filtered case was parsed into a structured JSON schema patient case that follows 3 main structures. Diagnosis related were made with Prompt 9.1 , treatment related were made with Prompt 9.1 and for Diagnosis and Treatment cases, the structure were made with Prompt 9.1 The model-facing version of each case masks the diagnostic answer by stripping the entire diagnosis section and, where applicable, the final-diagnosis field within the "presentation" field. Because the gold label or its acronyms occasionally surfaces inside "presentation" and "exams" (e.g. titles, framing sentences, or verdict clauses around investigations), we conducted a leakege verification that matches of the gold label and its acronym variants with [REDIGIDO] , and additionally drops verdict clauses gated on a long gold-token co-occurrence. 

3.2 Model panel 

• MedGemma-27B [39 ], is the text-only, 27-billion-parameter variant of Google’s open MedGemma collection, a dense decoder-only transformer built on the Gemma-3 backbone whose language component is further trained on a broad corpus of medical text and question–answer data. MedGemma is a collection of Gemma 3 variants trained for performance on medical text and image comprehension, making it our domain-specialised, English-centric reference point. • Sabiá-4 [ 40 ], is the latest closed-source model in Maritaca AI’s Sabiá family of Brazilian-Portuguese LLMs. It is a Transformer-based system whose exact parameter count is not publicly disclosed, developed through a multi-stage pipeline of continued pre-training on Portuguese and Brazilian legal corpora, long-context extension, instruction tuning, and preference alignment. • DeepSeek-R1 [ 41 ], is a 671-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts transformer post-trained with large-scale rule-based reinforcement learning to elicit explicit reasoning. • o3-mini [ 42 ], is a closed-source reasoning model from OpenAI’s o-series, included as a frontier closed-weight reasoning baseline. 3.3 Diagnosis Accuracy 

For the diagnosis task, each benchmark model receives a masked case consisting of only with the "presentation" and "exam" sections and is asked to return the most likely diagnosis considering the full case. Each prediction is then paired with the reference diagnsosis from the original case. The evaluation is done by a LLM-as-judge by gpt-oss-120b 

with the Prompt 9.3 that graded binary if it’s correct or incorrect with a short justification. The reported metric is the mean accuracy and the headline quantity is ∆( EN − PT ) with a paired per-case bootstrap 95% . Diagnosis was scored on n=2 ,135 cases paired across both languages. 

3.4 Differential diagnosis and Exam Recommendation 

For the differential diagnosis task, each benchmark model receives a masked case "diagnosis" and is asked to return The differential-diagnosis with the prompt 9.4. This prompt elicits a differential diagnoses — initial candidate list, systematic evaluation against the case, step-by-step exclusion, and a justified final diagnosis — and returns the candidate list as a structured array. The exam-recommendation task asks each model to recommend the diagnostic work-up for a masked case using the prompt 9.2. Acting as the ordering physician, the model lists the complementary tests it would request — constrained to be specific (e.g., "abdominal CT with contrast" rather than "imaging") and justified by the clinical picture, and returns them as a structured object grouped into four categories: laboratory, imaging, pathology, and other. 5Souza et al. The scoring proceeds by entity-linking both the model and the gold lists to the UMLS Metathesaurus [ 43 ]. Each diagnosis string is embedded with SapBERT-XLMR [ 44 ], a multilingual SAP-tuned encoder that places PT and EN surface forms in a common biomedical space, so EN predictions can be linked and matched by inner product against a FAISS-CPU IndexFlatIP index restricted to disease semantic types. Precision, Recall and F1 scores are computed over the resulting CUI sets, with depth-2 hierarchical credit: a predicted CUI counts as a hit if it is in the gold set, an ancestor of a gold CUI, or a descendant of a gold CUI under the UMLS CHD/RN relations. 

3.5 Treatment 

For the treatment task, each model receives a masked output and is asked to write a treatment plan in free-form. We selected 3 different judges ( Qwen-2.5-72B, LLaMA 3.3 70B and Gpt-oss-120b) was chosen to represent 3 types of model architechture and training, in order to decrease the bias for any given judgement. Every judge is shown the case, the proposed treatment, and the reference treatment, and returns three 1–5 scores: • Accuracy : whether the proposed interventions are clinically correct given the diagnosis and case context; • Completeness : whether the response covers the components present in the gold treatment • Clarity : whether the response is written in a clinically usable, standard format. The composite score reported is the mean of the three axes, divided by 5 so that it sits on a [0 , 1] scale comparable to the other tasks. 

3.6 Data Leakage 

The pre-training of all the models is extremelly broad, spanning across all the years that the related cases were published, what can cause the model to memorize the answers and achieve higher scores in the benchmark. To tackle this limitation, we conducted a standard year stratification test. For each model and language, we computed the main diagnosis accuracy for each year, between 2015 and 2024. Every accuracy measure is uniform across the four models.

# 4 Results 

4.1 The Benchmark 

ClinicalBr is a Brazilian Portuguese clinical decision benchmark built from the SciELO Online Library. This library covers open-access academic literature from countries across South America. In this work, we use the SciELO Brazil collection of Medicine Journals, which consists of several case-report-type papers containing real information about how real clinical cases were developed. The primary language of the papers is Portuguese, however, some journals are English-based, even when reporting Brazilian cases. The benchmark covers four stages of the clinical reasoning process: differential diagnosis, diagnosis retrieval, exam recommendation, and treatment planning. Each case has a complete version and a masked version according to the evaluation settings. For example, when evaluating diagnosis, the "diagnosis" section is removed from the structured case. Additionally, all cases are structured as parallel pairs in Portuguese and English to enable a clear cross-lingual comparison, the translation process is detailed in the methodology section. To construct the benchmark, we employ a 6ClinicalBr six-step pipeline that creates a final corpus of 2,892 cases from the SciELO medical journals, spanning more than 18 medical specialties. The corpus undergoes five filtering stages to ensure its coherence and reliability within the medical domain. 

4.1.1 Data Composition 

For each case in our benchmark, there is a structured file with four fields extracted from the original case-report-type paper. The body of the file is represented by: "presentation" (e.g, clinical data, patient data, family history, past medication), "exams" (e.g, laboratory, imaging), "diagnosis" (the accurate final diagnosis of the case), "treatment" (The treatment plan with their outcomes and intervention). Considering all of the cases, not all of them are focused on diagnosis or treatment, some have only one focused area. For the final evaluation setting the benchmark had 853 diagnosis-only cases, 744 treatment-only cases, and 1,303 cases tagged "both" (both diagnostic and therapeutic focused). Portuguese and English cases are equal by construction, the pipeline was the same, preserving all the information in the four-field structure (Table 1) 

4.1.2 Evaluation settings 

The benchmark is designed to be a open-ended questioning reasoning for the models. There are no closed questions. Each case is accompanied by the answer extracted in the original paper, which is labeled as the reference answer. In some cases, the reference answer is clear and direct, such as in the diagnosis retrieval, in other cases, the reference answer is used as a support to compare with the model predicted answer. The rationale is that in healthcare, treatment and exam recommendation rarely has a single correct answer. All evaluation is conducted in two separate language settings, Portuguese and english. A second evaluation is done specific to tropical countries such as Brazil. Table 4.2. The following are the specifications for each type of evaluation. • Diagnosis retrieval: The model receives a masked case, only the clinical presentation and exam results, with diagnosis and treatment removed. The reference answer is the diagnosis stated in the original paper. Performance is measured by accuracy against that reference. • Differential diagnosis: The model receives the same masked case. The reference answer is a differential diagnosis list generated by a model that had access to the full case. Performance is measured by comparison to that list. • Exam recommendation: The model receives the same masked case. The reference answer is the set of exams documented in the full case. Each predicted exam is mapped to a UMLS CUI using SapBERT-XLMR, and performance is measured by F1 against the reference set. • Treatment planning: The model receives the masked case with the diagnosis revealed and treatment removed. The reference answer is the treatment described in the original paper. Performance is scored by an LLM-as-judge on three rubric metrics: completeness, accuracy, and clarity. 

4.1.3 Evaluation metrics 

The model’s performance is evaluated through three domain-specific tasks. First, Diagnosis Accuracy is assessed as the accuracy of the predicted answer against the reference answer in the extracted diagnostic field, with a LLM-as-judge. The judge verifies for semantically similar diagnoses and scores with 1 or 0. Second, for Differential Diagnosis and Exam recommendation, we used feature extraction on the generated answer and match them with their UMLS CUI, then we matched the reference answer with the UMLS CUI. We then compare with 3 metrics: precision, recall, and F1. Finally, for Treatment Planning, a mean of three LLM-as-judge scores spanning 1–5 on a Likert scale (accuracy, completeness, and clarity) is normalized to a [0,1] scale. Treatment Accuracy measures the factual correctness of recommended elements, acting as a precision metric when comparing to the reference answer . Treatment Completeness functions as a recall metric for the treatment space, comparing to the ground truth response. 4.5 Treatment Planning 

Figure 5: Treatment subscores The treatment evaluation constitutes the final component of our benchmark assessment. In this task, LLM judges evaluate open-ended responses rather than applying a binary right-or-wrong check. Because relying on a single judge can introduce systematic bias and variations stemming from model training, we employed three distinct judges: Qwen-2.5-72B, Llama-3.3-70B, and gpt-oss-120B. These judges scored responses across three dimensions: accuracy, completeness, and clarity. In total, the panel generated 16,300 scores. We present these results as a 3 × 3 grid in Figure 5, from which three primary findings emerge. First, o3-mini achieved the highest accuracy, reaching a consensus mean of 0.766. However, it fell short in completeness, dropping to 0.674 and ranking third behind Sabiá-4 and DeepSeek-R1. Consistent with our observations in the differential diagnosis task, the model that generates the most clinically accurate treatments is not necessarily the one that produces the most comprehensive plan. Second, DeepSeek-R1 exhibited a contrasting performance profile. It scored highly in completeness, with a mean of 0.661, but ranked second in accuracy. It consistently generated longer treatment plans that incorporated diverse clinical perspectives. Third, the clarity metric failed to differentiate the models significantly, all evaluated models produced clear, well-organized plans. This uniformity reflects a recurrent bias: when models generate longer outputs, judges tend to award higher clarity scores. Conversely, the primary drivers of low clarity scores were the inclusion of vague medical jargon and disorganized formatting. Notably, accuracy and completeness exhibited the strongest dependence on the reference treatment, which also corresponded to the greatest variance among judge scores. Disagreements between the model and 10 ClinicalBr the reference treatment most frequently occurred when the model failed to predict the identical drug or a highly relevant component of the treatment plan. When analyzing cross-lingual performance, accuracy scores remained closely matched, with models scoring between 0.72 and 0.77 in both languages. The absolute difference between English and Portuguese performance fell within 

±0.005 , and every confidence interval included zero. For completeness, models scored between 0.62 and 0.66 in Portuguese, compared to 0.59 to 0.65 in English. While three of the four models demonstrated a nominal advantage in Portuguese, this variance was not statistically significant. # 5 Discussion 

Cross-lingual gaps between language models can become a safety concern when they are utilized in a clinical decision support tool. Language models are used daily to provide accessible care information and support for users and healthcare workers, but when it comes to their implementation in critical scenarios, language performance needs to be evaluated. Our results showed that across all four models, English language diagnosis retrieval was better than Portuguese by a margin of more than 10% in most cases (MedGemma-27B +0.121 [+0.099, +0.143]; Sabiá-4 +0.107 [+0.086, +0.128]; DeepSeek-R1 +0.075 [+0.053, +0.096]; o3-mini +0.081 [+0.061, +0.104]), and the confidence interval excluded zero in every case. This gap was the largest for the more training language-anchored models, MedGemma-27B and Sabiá-4, and the gap was smallest for the reasoning models, DeepSeek-R1 and o3-mini. This raises a question: most hospitals and care institutions only have data in their own language about their patients, and translation can be a hard task, what does that mean for the millions of patients across the country? 

The cross-lingual difference stops when the task is not only accuracy. When evaluation changed from simple accuracy to other metrics, such as Recall and F1, there was no longer a practical English advantage. For differential diagnosis reasoning, the difference between the languages’ confidence interval crossed zero in 3 of 4 models. When it comes to exam selection, the results showed a slight advantage across some models for Portuguese. Reading again the first diagnosis claim, when it comes to a precise final answer, the English language has a clear advantage that does not generalize to tasks that require more reasoning and recalling more than one correct answer. 

Models can recall the right diagnosis without being able to prioritize them. As stated in the last paragraph, all models perform well at gathering the most probable answers regardless of language. In the differential diagnosis evaluation, recall ranked against the suggested answer was high, in the range of 0.8, but when it comes to ranking with the correct diagnosis first, the F1 drops to 0.20–0.27. This matters for deployment in real clinical settings: the model has a great ability to recall all the possible answers, but the human-in-the-loop factor is still imperative to maintain safety for the final clinical decision. On top of that, reasoning models tend to be more precise in terms of the correct final diagnosis. 

Using AI to recommend medical tests is where current models are the furthest away from actually being useful for healthcare teams. The F1 score never exceeded 0.1 among all of the models in any language, less than half of the differential diagnosis ceiling. Against an extracted standard from the cases, with an average of 6.6 exams, the models that proposed a higher number of exams tended to score higher. Sabiá-4 proposed 19.1, DeepSeek-R1 13.6, and 12 ClinicalBr MedGemma-27B 10.3, while only o3-mini stayed in the clinically plausible range at 5.6. o3-mini performed best on precision by ordering conservatively. In a real clinical scenario, particularly in underdeveloped countries, more exams means more money the patient would have to spend; in these cases, models that recommend more exams increase the cognitive load of the practitioner and can decrease their efficiency. 

Brazilian-endemic conditions were easier, not harder, for models. We anticipated that tropical and neglected diseases that are less represented in English-centric corpora would expose more errors in the clinical decision benchmark. However, the opposite held: every model in both languages scored as well on the 219-case subset as on the full corpus. The largest gains were in Portuguese diagnosis accuracy (+7.9 points for Sabiá-4, +6.1 for DeepSeek-R1, +4.8 for o3-mini). Tropical diseases such as dengue, leishmaniasis, and Chagas are well documented in global English and biomedical text, so they are well represented in pre-training. 

The treatment-task language difference is an artifact of the judge, not a property of the models. All treatment evaluation used three judges. Under Qwen, Portuguese plans scored higher, but this did not survive the switch to Llama-3.3-70B or the stricter gpt-oss-120B. Pooled into a consensus, the gap collapses: accuracy stays within ±0.005 between languages with every confidence interval crossing zero, and the small Portuguese edge in completeness (0.62–0.66 vs. 0.59–0.65) is not significant for any model. As Figure 7 shows, the treatment deltas cluster on the zero line, with only MedGemma-27B’s completeness leaning toward Portuguese, and as the English-centric model, it is the least likely to drive a true Portuguese advantage, confirming this residual as judge variance. The implication is methodological: on open-ended tasks, a single-judge protocol would have reported a Portuguese advantage that a multi-judge protocol reveals as an artifact of one grader’s scoring tendencies. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduce ClinicalBr, the first bilingual benchmark for clinical reasoning built from real Brazilian Case Reports. Spanning across more than eighteen specialties and structured as Portuguese and English pairs, it evaluated frontier models on diagnosis retrieval, differential diagnosis, exam recommendation and treatment planning under a design framework that distinguishes case difficulty from the language effect. Our central finding is that the difference between the languages is a matter of knowledge retrieval and not reasoning of cases. English is the language that improves the best answers for single accuracy on diagnosis. Weaker models benefit the most from switching to English because they rely more on retrieval when compared to reasoning models that rely on the reasoning process before the answer. Yet, this advantage disappears the moment the task shifts to more structured reasoning for recommendations in the case. Differential diagnosis, exam selection, and treatment planning show Portuguese to be at least the equal of English, and Brazilian-endemic conditions proved easier rather than harder despite being the content least represented in English-centric corpora.

7.1 Qualitative Case Study
